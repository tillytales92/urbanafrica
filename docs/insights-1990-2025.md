# Insights — 1990–2025 (8-epoch rebuild)

**Status:** findings notes, 2026-09-08. Numbers pulled from the rebuilt
`data/intermediate/` `.Rds` (`africapolis_builtup.Rds`, `africapolis_pop.Rds`,
`city_index.Rds`, `sprawl_stats.Rds`, `africapolis_ntl.Rds`) after the
8-epoch / 1990-headline migration. Supersedes the data in
`docs/urban-growth-stories.md` (that was the 6-epoch / 2000-headline build).

Scope: the **100 largest African agglomerations** by Africapolis 2020 population.
Built-up surface + population: **GHSL BUILT-S / GHS-POP R2023A, eight epochs
1990–2025**. Nighttime lights: **bloom- & top-coding-corrected DMSP** (Chiovelli,
Michalopoulos, Papaioannou & Regan 2026), annual 1992–2025 — but NTL series are
read from **2000** onward here (pre-2000 corrected DMSP is noisier).

---

## Data caveats (carry these wherever the number appears)

1. **Small secondary-city % / absolute outliers are partly polygon artifacts.**
   Kikima (KEN) reads **+1116 %** (11 → 138 km²) on only +51 % population — an
   Africapolis boundary capturing scattered rural settlement. Treat Kikima as
   noise. Sodo, Hawassa, Nakuru, Embu are directionally real (fast-growing
   Ethiopian/Kenyan towns) but their exact % should be read with caution.
2. **Kisumu and Port Harcourt are excluded** from the top-100 set (see the app's
   About tab) — the next-ranked agglomerations take their slots.
3. **Africapolis boundaries are a fixed vintage.** Cities that sprawled past
   their polygon edge (Kinshasa the clearest case) are undercounted — their low
   *measured* area growth is partly a static-boundary artifact.
4. **GHS-POP is disaggregated using the built-up layer**, so any per-capita
   built-up density from the two is partly circular. Directional, not precise.
5. **NTL "dark-or-dim → lit" transitions** (§5) are large and abrupt for many
   cities. Some is real electrification; some is the corrected-DMSP series'
   sensitivity near its floor. Read as directional.
6. **Luanda's 1990 population density reads implausibly low** (459 persons/km²
   of built-up land, vs. 780+ for every other city in the sample) and therefore
   its 1990→2025 density increase (+2,455%, by far the largest in the sample)
   should be read as a data-quality artifact, not a real 25x densification —
   treat like Kikima (caveat 1): flag, don't headline.
7. **Abuja's growth number is itself likely an undercount.** A boundary-
   saturation check (edge-vs-interior built-up fill inside each Africapolis
   polygon, `scripts/exploratory/1_edge_saturation_check.R` →
   `data/intermediate/edge_saturation_check.Rds`) found Abuja's polygon nearly
   wall-to-wall built-up right up to its border (edge/interior fill ratio
   **1.04**, the highest of any scorable city) — on top of already having the
   single largest growth figure in the sample (+404%, 1990–2025). The §3
   "deceleration" read on Abuja may partly be a measurement ceiling, not a
   genuine slowdown. Ten other cities show the same pattern more mildly
   (Agadir, Johannesburg, Durban, Constantine, Cape Town, Onitsha, Nsukka,
   Al-Iskandariya, Bafoussam, Harare). Cairo could not be tested at all
   (geometry too complex to run the check). The other 89 scored cities,
   including every other megacity (Lagos, Kinshasa, Nairobi, Kigali, Abidjan,
   Accra), show no such signal — their growth numbers aren't boundary-capped.

## Findings

### 1. Land grew far slower than people
- Built-up footprint (top-100): **6,906 → 12,502 km²  (+81 %)**, 1990 → 2025.
- Population (top-100): **142 M → 370 M  (+161 %)**.
- Median built-up land per million residents: **48 → 35 km²** — it *fell*.
- Only **26 of 100** cities had footprint outrun population (ratio > 1).
- Takeaway: at the aggregate this era was **densifying, not sprawling** — the
  opposite of the usual story.

### 2. Two distinct worlds
- The genuine **sprawl frontier is Ethiopian & Kenyan secondary cities** —
  Hawassa, Sodo, Nakuru, Embu — growing 200–600 % in area on 50–160 % more
  people; their density roughly **halved**.
- Most sprawl-dominated (sprawl share of growth, min 30 km² growth):
  Kikima 95 %, Sodo 92 %, Nakuru 91 %, Hawassa 85 %, Benin City 82 %, Embu 81 %,
  Ikorodu 79 %, Abuja 76 %, Nouakchott 75 %, Khartoum 73 %.

### 3. East Africa is the engine; Central Africa woke up late
- Median built-up % growth 1990–2025: **East 109 · West 100 · North 81 ·
  Central 48 · Southern 45**. East + West added ~3,600 of the ~5,600 km² total.
- Median CAGR: East 2.1 %, West 2.0 %, North 1.7 %, Central 1.1 %, Southern 1.1 %.
- **Timing (only visible with the 1990 baseline):**
  - **Accelerated** (late-window rate ≫ early-window): Brazzaville
    (+4 % in 1990–2005 → +27 % in 2010–2025), Pointe-Noire (+4 → +22),
    Lubumbashi (+29 → +46), Libreville (+9 → +20), Benin City, Abeokuta, Dakar.
    → **Congo-basin cities flat in the 1990s, took off after 2010.**
  - **Decelerated** (early boom, late slowdown): Abuja (**+159 % → +42 %**),
    Lomé (+81 → +15), Kumasi (+76 → +27), Maiduguri (+64 → +16), Serrekunda,
    Evaton, Kampala, Mogadishu. → Abuja's new-capital build-out front-loaded
    (but see caveat 7 — Abuja's polygon is now boundary-saturated, so part of
    this "slowdown" may be a measurement ceiling, not a true deceleration).

### 4. Sprawl vs. infill splits by region
- **Most infill-dominated (lowest sprawl share):** Tunis 20 %, Algiers 20 %,
  Casablanca 30 %, Durban 33 %, Onitsha 34 %, Lomé 38 %, Kano 41 %, Cape Town 43 %.
  North African cities grew **almost entirely by filling in**.
- **Biggest densifiers** (density index Δ, surface ÷ footprint): Lomé +0.107,
  Abuja +0.099, Constantine +0.090, Maiduguri +0.088, Katsina +0.085,
  Oran +0.074, Damietta +0.073, Algiers +0.072.
- **Biggest thinning** (footprint outran surface): Hawassa −0.083, Nakuru −0.072,
  Sodo −0.062, Dakar −0.058, Kinshasa −0.055, Benin City −0.052, Conakry −0.048.

### 5. Lighting caught up dramatically (2000 → 2025)
- Biggest fall in dark-or-dim share (DN ≤ 10, share of built-up):
  Mogadishu 94 → 3 %, Ikorodu 86 → 2 %, Mwanza 85 → 2 %, Monrovia 98 → 17 %,
  Touba 80 → 0 %, Serrekunda 92 → 13 %, Hargeisa 78 → 0 %, Kumasi 74 → 0 %,
  Dar es Salaam 72 → 1 %, Luanda 71 → 0 %.
- Still most under-lit in 2025: Nsukka 99 %, Sodo 98 %, Kikima 98 %, Embu 96 %,
  Bafoussam 96 %, Hawassa 95 %.
- Takeaway: **the sprawl frontier is also the unlit frontier** — the same small
  East African / south-eastern Nigerian cities top both lists.

### 6. Megacities barely spread
- **Kinshasa +17 %** area (146 → 170 km²), **Mbuji-Mayi +14 %** — despite huge
  population growth. Intensifying informally inside a near-saturated footprint
  (and see caveat 3 on boundaries).
- Slowest 8 by % growth are almost all Central + Southern: Mbuji-Mayi 14,
  Kinshasa 17, Pointe-Noire 32, Port Elizabeth 33, Cape Town 35, Onitsha 38,
  Brazzaville 38, Luanda 39.
- Biggest **absolute** additions: Cairo +460 km² (→ 897), Embu +236 (artifact),
  Johannesburg +207 (→ 674), Lagos +195 (→ 489), Tripoli +169, Accra +158.

### 7. Cities are sprawling out, not building up
- One-off check, 2000 vs. 2025 only (GHS-BUILT-V volume ÷ BUILT-S footprint =
  mean building height; not run for the other epochs). Median mean height
  across the top-100 **fell from 7.67 m to 7.25 m** — not one city gained
  more than 0.5 m in 25 years.
- Footprint growth (BUILT-S, median +48%) consistently outran volume growth
  (BUILT-V, median +36%): the "vertical share" of growth is negative on
  average (median **−29%**) — arithmetically, most volume growth is coming
  from spreading out, not up.
- Biggest height losses: Nakuru −2.38 m, Sodo −2.17 m, Hawassa −2.05 m,
  Tanger −1.91 m, Bujumbura −1.53 m, Al-Iskandariya −1.47 m, Al-Qahira
  −1.39 m, Kigali −1.36 m, Abuja −1.24 m, Casablanca −1.21 m.
- Takeaway: reinforces §1/§2 — new construction is overwhelmingly low-rise
  horizontal expansion at the edge, diluting citywide average height even
  where downtown cores may be getting taller. No case for building a full
  continental BUILT-V pipeline off this signal; the headline number stands
  on its own.

### 8. Growth trajectories cluster into three characteristic types

- Two independent tags, both tercile splits so they self-calibrate to the
  sample rather than using fixed cutoffs (now surfaced in the app's Rankings
  table and Urban Growth info box):
  - **Growth timing** — late-window rate (2010–2025) minus early-window rate
    (1990–2005); Accelerating / Steady / Decelerating. Requires a 1990 base
    > 20 km² (22 cities too small to classify).
  - **Growth form** — sprawl share of 1990–2025 growth; Sprawl-dominant /
    Mixed / Infill-dominant. Requires > 30 km² total growth (46 cities too
    slow-growing to classify).
- Crossing the two shows growth isn't spread evenly across all nine
  combinations — cities cluster on the diagonal: of the 47 cities with a
  defined value on both tags, 27 (57%) fall into one of three matching
  timing/form pairs. Conditional on having a defined form:

  | Timing | Infill-dominant | Mixed | Sprawl-dominant |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Accelerating (n=12 scored) | 8% | 42% | **50%** |
  | Steady (n=17 scored) | 29% | **53%** | 18% |
  | Decelerating (n=18 scored) | **67%** | 22% | 11% |

- Read as a **maturation pattern**: cities still in an active land-grabbing
  expansion phase are sprawling and currently accelerating; cities whose
  sprawl phase happened earlier are now infilling and decelerating because
  the easy greenfield is gone. Checked against two obvious confounders before
  trusting it: it is **not** the boundary-saturation artifact (only 2 of the
  12 Decelerating+Infill-dominant cities are boundary-saturated — see caveat
  7 — and Abuja, the boundary-saturation poster child, is itself
  Decelerating+**Sprawl**-dominant, the opposite corner) and **not** a city-size
  artifact (median 1990 footprint is ~50 km² in both the Accelerating and
  Decelerating groups).
- **Sprawling & accelerating** (median +157% growth, 75% sprawl share, n=6):
  almost entirely the Nigerian periphery still expanding into greenfield, plus
  one Kenyan secondary city — **Lagos**, **Ikorodu** (a Lagos exurb in its own
  right, +261%), **Benin City**, **Embu** (Kenya).
- **Infilling & decelerating** (median +81% growth, 42% sprawl share, n=12):
  established regional capitals/metros whose expansion boom is behind them —
  **Johannesburg**, **Accra**, **Tunis**, **Kampala** (one from each of
  Southern, West, North and East Africa; no Central African city appears in
  this group — consistent with §3's "Central Africa woke up late").
- **Steady & mixed** (median +96% growth, 59% sprawl share, n=9): a balanced
  middle path, still growing at a roughly constant rate with growth split
  between infill and new land — **Al-qahira (Cairo)**, **Nairobi**,
  **Bamako**, **Yaoundé**.
- **"Infilling" here means flat, not tall.** Cross-referencing §7's per-city
  mean-height change (BUILT-V ÷ BUILT-S, 2000 vs. 2025 only) against the
  growth-form tag: Infill-dominant cities lose height more slowly than
  Sprawl-dominant ones (median &minus;0.41 m vs. &minus;0.79 m; correlation
  between height change and sprawl share is &minus;0.42, the expected sign)
  but their median is still **negative** — only 1 of 18 Infill-dominant
  cities (Lomé, +0.01 m, effectively noise) gained any height at all. So
  "mature" cities that have stopped sprawling outward are not building
  upward to compensate; they are filling in previously-empty or
  partially-built land within their existing footprint, not adding floors.
  The §8 maturation story is a maturation into density, not height.
- Caveat: this is a cross-sectional correlation between two metrics both
  derived from the same GHSL time series, not a causal claim about *why*
  cities decelerate when they infill — a genuine test would need a
  time-lagged design (does infill precede deceleration, or vice versa) that
  this one-off cross-tab doesn't attempt.

### 9. Population densities rose almost everywhere — confirming §1 from a second, independent metric

- Population density here = GHS-POP ÷ **built-up footprint** (persons per km²
  of built-up land, not per km² of the whole, often-empty Africapolis
  polygon) — the same denominator as the built-up density index in §4. Now
  live in the app (Rankings → Population; Urban Growth info box).
- Top-100 aggregate (population-weighted): **3,702 → 4,990 persons/km²
  (+35%)**, 1990 → 2025. Median city-level: **4,067 → 5,903 (+40%)**.
- **82 of 100 cities densified**; only 18 thinned out (pop density fell) —
  and those 18 are almost exactly the §2 sprawl frontier: Kikima (artifact,
  caveat 1), Sodo −87%, Hawassa −86%, Nakuru −68%, Embu −53%, plus Gisenyi/
  Goma, Benin City, Touba, Ikorodu, Pointe-Noire, Juba, Kigali (−18% to −29%).
- **Growth-form tag (§8) cleanly separates the two stories**: median
  pop-density change is **+68% for Infill-dominant** cities and **+62% for
  Mixed**, but only **−12% for Sprawl-dominant** cities (terciles, n=18
  each). A second, independently-derived metric (population, not built-up
  surface/footprint) lands on the same "densifying, not sprawling" story as
  §1 — reassuring, since §1's built-up density index and this pop-density
  figure share no common data source beyond the footprint denominator.
- **Central Africa densified fastest of any region** (median pop-density
  change **+85%**, vs. North +30%, South +63%, West +40%, East +27%) —
  despite having the *slowest* median land growth of any region (§3, +48%).
  This is the same "megacities barely spread" cities from §6, now with a
  number attached: **Mbuji-Mayi's** pop density more than doubled to become
  the single highest in the sample (**14,230 → 34,043**/km², 2025), and
  **Kinshasa's** nearly tripled (**8,158 → 24,727**). Population growth that
  couldn't find new footprint (§6, +14–17%) went into the existing one
  instead.
- **East Africa has the lowest pop density of any region in both years**
  (2,831 → 3,309, +27%) despite being §3's growth engine (highest median
  built-up % growth) — consistent with §2: East Africa's growth was
  disproportionately the Ethiopian/Kenyan secondary-city sprawl frontier,
  which grows by spreading, not filling in.
- Highest 2025 densities: Mbuji-Mayi 34,043, Al-Uqsur (Luxor) 27,438,
  Mogadishu 27,287, Kinshasa 24,727, Al-Mansura/Mahalla al-Kubra 16,559,
  Douala 16,491 persons/km² of built-up land.
- Caveat: see caveat 4 (GHS-POP/GHSL circularity) and caveat 6 (Luanda's
  implausible 1990 base).

## Literature connections

Two IGC pieces landed in the project folder in September 2026 — Delbridge,
Harman, Oliveira-Cunha & Venables (2022), *Sustainable urbanisation in
developing countries: Cities as places to live* (IGC Growth Brief), and
Bryan, Glaeser & Tsivanidis (2024), *Cities* (IGC Evidence Paper, a survey of
the economics-of-cities literature in low- and middle-income countries).
Neither uses this dataset, but both connect directly to what it measures.

- **The "constant density" scenario didn't happen.** Bryan et al. cite Angel
  (2011): *if density levels remain the same in African cities, urban land
  cover in 2050 will be four times higher than it was in 2000* — a
  hypothetical used to warn about unchecked sprawl. This dataset gives an
  actual 1990–2025 answer for the top-100: density did **not** stay flat —
  it rose in aggregate (§1: built-up land per million residents fell 48 → 35
  km²; §9: median pop density +40%). The Angel scenario describes the
  handful of cities that *are* roughly constant-density — the §2/§9 sprawl
  frontier (Hawassa, Sodo, Nakuru, Embu) — not the sample as a whole.
- **Bryan et al. flag "are cities in low- and middle-income countries
  sprawling inefficiently? What policies would improve land use?" as an open
  research priority.** The §8 growth-timing/growth-form typology (Sprawl-
  dominant/Accelerating vs. Infill-dominant/Decelerating, with a
  "maturation" reading) and this dataset's full sprawl/infill decomposition
  per city and year are a direct, ready-made empirical input to that
  question — 100 cities, panel, not cross-sectional.
- **Methodological validation.** Bryan et al. note that "machine vision
  techniques have opened the possibility of using daytime satellite imagery
  to measure the size and density of slums... and urban areas" (Gechter &
  Tsivanidis 2023; Vogel et al. 2019) as the frontier for LMIC urban data.
  This project's core method — GHSL built-up + corrected-DMSP nighttime
  lights as an unlit/dim proxy for informal or low-density settlement (§5,
  and the app's lit/unlit layer) — is squarely in that tradition, just
  applied continent-wide and across 35 years rather than one city.
- **"Expanding land cover rather than building higher density" — directly
  confirmed by §7.** Bryan et al. state this as the general LMIC pattern;
  §7's one-off BUILT-V check (median mean building height falling 7.67 m →
  7.25 m across the top-100, 2000–2025) is a direct, independently-derived
  confirmation of that claim for this specific sample.
- **Delbridge et al.'s policy case for density (cheaper services, lower
  emissions) is a useful lens for reading §9, not a caveat on it.** They cite
  per-person service costs roughly 10% lower for water, 20% for power, and
  50% for roads in dense vs. rural areas, and note Kigali's waste generation
  growing at 4x its population growth rate. The cities this dataset finds
  densifying fastest — Kinshasa, Mbuji-Mayi, and Central Africa generally
  (§9) — are absorbing population into a static footprint with, on the
  available evidence, the least fiscal capacity in the sample (§3: Central
  Africa the slowest and latest-starting region) to extend the
  infrastructure that makes density a benefit rather than a strain. The
  brief's framing suggests **whether** these cities' rapid densification
  shows up as a benefit or a strain is an infrastructure/service-delivery
  question this built-up/population dataset alone cannot answer — worth
  flagging as a limitation of the "densifying = good" reading of §1/§9,
  not extending the dataset to try to answer it.
- Neither paper is about Africa exclusively, and neither analyses satellite
  built-up data directly — connections here are conceptual, not a shared
  data source, and shouldn't be presented as validating this project's
  numbers.
